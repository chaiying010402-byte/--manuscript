Its limitation was noted early: a term's position in an aggregated map says little about how the term is used where it occurs (Leydesdorff, 1997), and semantic structure differs across levels of textual aggregation, from words to sentences, paragraphs and sections (Leydesdorff & Welbers, 2011). Computational linguistics has developed dedicated methods for tracing changes in a word's usage, chiefly diachronic word embeddings and, more recently, contextualised models (Hamilton et al., 2016; see Tahmasebi et al., 2021, for a survey). Embedding-based comparisons across periods, however, are sensitive to modelling choices and can yield artefacts unless carefully controlled (Dubossarsky et al., 2017; Shoemark et al., 2019), and multiword technical expressions require additional representational decisions. We therefore adopt a transparent, count-based distributional approach: following the principle that a word's usage is characterised by the company it keeps (Firth, 1957), the local context of a focal expression is described by its collocates, each traceable to concordance (KWIC) lines (Sinclair, 1991), while the same expressions are followed through prevalence and co-occurrence networks. We study this question in research at the junction of EEG, fNIRS, brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) and human factors/ergonomics (HF/E), which neuroergonomics, the study of the brain in relation to performance at work and in everyday settings, has brought together (Parasuraman, 2003; Parasuraman & Rizzo, 2007; Mehta & Parasuraman, 2013). It is a suitable case because measurement modalities (Wolpaw et al., 1991; Villringer et al., 1993), cognitive constructs, computational methods and application goals such as workload assessment, neuroadaptive systems and assistive communication (Parasuraman & Wilson, 2008; Wolpaw et al., 2002) come from different traditions, so shared labels may be used differently across them, as the trading-zone account would lead us to expect. Bibliometric studies of adjacent areas have mapped publication trends, thematic structure, and keyword co-occurrence at the aggregate level, including BCI research topics and knowledge structure (Yin et al., 2023), pre/post-2018 shifts in BCI review vocabulary (Alharbi, 2023), bibliometric and keyword-network analyses of BCI research (Angulo Medina et al., 2024), and thematic and co-word structure in neurofeedback research (Wider et al., 2024). Based on a targeted rather than systematic search, we did not find a study that jointly tracks the sentence-level, KWIC-traceable collocational change of fixed technical expressions and the reorganisation of the co-occurrence network linking those same expressions. As an illustration, our data show _workload_ keeping the same label throughout the corpus while its local context shifts: the expressions most strongly associated with it move from general psychological vocabulary toward measurement- and application-specific companions such as _EEG_, _usability_, and _auditory_ (Section 4.4). The label persists; what it keeps company with does not.

Our data are the English abstracts of publications by a purposive sample of 175 highly visible, institutionally central scholars (170 resolved to an OpenAlex author profile) assembled from professional-society leadership lists, conference-committee rosters and journal editorial boards spanning EEG and neuroscience, fNIRS, EEG–fNIRS and multimodal research, industrial engineering and manufacturing, theory-bridging scholarship, and methods and statistics, supplemented by additional researchers relevant to the review's scope.
